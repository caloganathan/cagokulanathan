## What the foam is telling you

A fungal biopesticide tank that foams at sporulation is usually getting thicker. Viscosity changes mixing. Mixing changes the oxygen the culture actually receives, which is not the oxygen your rotameter says you sent. The culture responds with more foam, more incomplete sporulation, or both. Operators then add antifoam, which knocks the foam down and can make oxygen transfer worse. The batch looks calmer and the spore count does not recover.

The useful record is dull: time, airflow or rpm, a viscosity note even if it is a cup and a stopwatch, foam height, and the antifoam additions. Three batches of that record are more diagnostic than a new sensor bought in a hurry. If you already have dissolved oxygen, plot it on the same page. The hour where DO collapses and foam rises is the hour to redesign, not the whole recipe.

## Media, oxygen and the 200–1,000 L window

Flask results lie politely. Shaking a flask is not a sparger in a tank. When the volume moves into a few hundred litres, heat removal, the cold spot during SIP, and the way foam holds cells in the headspace all change. This is the window where our founder has resolved sporulation viscosity and foam. It is also the window where a recipe copied from 10 L, with the same antifoam millilitres per litre, stops making sense.

Media is the other half. A richer broth can raise biomass and then punish you with viscosity just as the fungus should be making spores. Starving a foam problem by cutting the carbon source can also cut the spore. The change has to be tried on a batch you are willing to instrument, not argued from a whiteboard.

We do not publish setpoints. Aeration, agitation, dissolved oxygen and pH are the levers named because they are the ones to measure. The number belongs to your organism, your geometry and your media. A number copied from this article would be a fabricated fact.

## Low spore yield after the foam is gone

Sometimes the foam is fixed and the spore is still poor. Look at the harvest criterion. Teams harvest on a clock because the downstream shift starts at a clock. Sporulation is not a clock. If the microscope and the count say the culture is early, a calmer tank will not invent spores.

Also look at what happens after harvest. A good spore killed in a hot blender or on a wet carrier will be reported as a fermentation failure. Split the count: end of fermentation, after formulation, after two weeks. The step that loses the log is the step you own.

Bacillus and fungal systems do not foam for identical reasons. Do not move a fungal antifoam story onto a bacterial biofertiliser without a new diagnosis. An approved bacterial example from our founder's work is separate: 100 L PGPR production that improved plant salinity tolerance by 20%. That is not a spore-yield claim.

## Solid-state is not an escape

If the stirred tank is the wrong machine, solid-state fermentation may fit the fungus and the product form. It brings its own uneven moisture and its own contamination paths. Switching mode to avoid a foam chart is how plants buy a second set of problems. Choose the mode from the organism, the form you must sell, and whether you can run a real SIP.
