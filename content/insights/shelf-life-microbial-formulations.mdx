The shelf life of a microbial formulation is the time during which it still meets the specification on the label, in the pack you sell, in a temperature the product will actually see. Moisture, the carrier, contaminants that ride along, and the formulation type — wettable powder, suspension concentrate, liquid or an encapsulated form — decide that time. A harvest count is not a shelf-life result. We do not publish an extension percentage for Beauveria or any other organism on this page. That figure has not been confirmed for disclosure.

## Separate the two counts

Every argument we see on a plant floor starts with one number. "We had 10 to the 8 at harvest." The farmer, the state laboratory and the buyer meet a different number, months later, in a pack that has been on a truck. If you only measure the first number, you will keep "fixing" the fermenter for a problem that starts at blending.

Take three counts on the next three batches: end of production, after formulation and packing, and at a planned intermediate age. You do not need a two-year answer to learn that the product loses a log in the first fortnight. That fortnight is usually water, heat, or a contaminant that was invisible on day one.

## Carriers and moisture

A spore or a cell on a dry, clean carrier is a different product from the same organism on a carrier that arrived with its own microbial load and its own water activity. Talc, charcoal, lignite and the various branded carriers are not interchangeable just because they are powders. The test is small and dull: moisture of the incoming carrier, a plate of the carrier before you add the culture, and the same tests after blending.

Wettable powders fail when they are not dry enough, or when they cake and the farmer mixes a lump that is not the specification. Liquids fail when the cells sediment, when the preservative system is wishful, or when the bottle breathes. Suspension concentrates fail in the gap between those two stories. Encapsulation is a way to slow the exchange of water and to protect a cell from a neighbour. It is a process with its own contamination risks, not a word to put on a brochure because the powder failed.

For Beauveria bassiana the same logic applies, with extra attention to the spore's sensitivity to moisture and heat. Our founder has done carrier and formulation work on that problem. We are not putting a percentage improvement on it until the number, the method and the right to publish it are confirmed.

## Contaminants masquerading as shelf life

A batch that "dies" is sometimes a batch that was overgrown. Your organism's count falls because something else rose. If you only plate the target, you will call it a stability failure and add a more expensive carrier. Plate the contaminants you already worry about, on the same calendar as the viability test.

This is also why an antibiotic sprinkled into a biological to "hold the count" is a bad shelf-life strategy. It admits the pack is not a controlled system, and it is a difficult sentence to defend in front of a buyer who asked for an antibiotic-free input.

## What the study has to look like

Match the pack, the fill volume and the temperature to the real chain. Include a hot condition if the product will sit in a warehouse that is hot. Define the method, the dilution and the person who is allowed to read the plate. A shelf-life claim that depends on one unreplicated bottle is a hope.

If the date you want and the date the data support are different, change the date. Printing the commercial date and "monitoring trade complaints" is not a study.

## Where this sits in a registration file

For a biostimulant, a biofertiliser or a biopesticide, the date on the label is a claim. The dossier or the quality specification has to be able to live with it. We will not use a stability graph to prop up a provisional biostimulant registration. Those registrations have been invalid since 17 Jun 2025. Shelf life does not revive them.
